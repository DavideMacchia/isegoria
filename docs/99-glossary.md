# Part 1 — How judgments are aggregated

## Sybil attack

**Problem.** In an open network, anyone can create identities. An actor creates ten
thousand accounts and votes as it pleases. Any system that counts votes, or computes
averages, or measures consensus, is vulnerable to this.

**Name.** Coined by John Douceur in 2002, from the title of a book about dissociative
identity disorder.

**Why it is the main constraint.** All reputation math assumes that behind each ID
there is a distinct entity. If that is not true, the numbers keep coming out but no
longer measure anything. It is why in the specification identity comes before
everything else, even though we then moved it out of scope.

---

## Matrix factorization

**Problem.** You have a huge, mostly-empty table: the rows are reviewers, the columns
questions, the cells votes. Almost all cells are empty, because each reviewer sees only
a handful of items. You want to understand the underlying structure.

**Intuition.** The hypothesis is that votes are not arbitrary but generated by a few
"hidden dimensions". It is the same mechanism as Netflix recommendations: nobody tells
the system that Marco likes action films. The system infers it from the fact that
Marco's votes correlate with those of other people, and discovers by itself an axis
along which people arrange themselves. You do not give it the dimensions: it finds
them.

**Mathematically.** You approximate the vote matrix `R` (n rows × m columns) as the
product of two much smaller matrices: `U` (n × d) and `V` (m × d), with `d` small — 1,
2, at most 5. Each reviewer becomes a vector `f_u` of `d` numbers. Each question
becomes a vector `f_j`.

**Why we need it.** Because that vector `f_u` is, in practice, the reviewer's
ideological position — measured from behavior, never declared, and never linked to a
real person.

---

## The model, piece by piece

```
r̂_uj = μ + b_u + b_j + ⟨f_u , f_j⟩
```

Read it thus: "the predicted vote of reviewer *u* on question *j* is the sum of four
contributions".

| Symbol | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `μ` | overall mean of all votes | "on average questions get 0.6" |
| `b_u` | how generous or severe that reviewer is *in general* | someone who rates everything high has positive `b_u` |
| `b_j` | how much that question is liked **regardless of who judges it**, in the fit | reported; the score the gate reads is the side-balanced approval below |
| `⟨f_u, f_j⟩` | the part of the vote explained by ideological alignment | reviewer and question "on the same side" → positive contribution |

`b_u` serves to avoid rewarding an item just because it landed with generous
reviewers. The final score is the *side-balanced approval* (`02` §A.3): the reviewers
are split into two sides along `f`, the model's predicted votes are averaged on each
side, and the two averages are averaged again — so each side counts once, whatever its
size, and the score lives on the same scale as the votes.

**Dot product `⟨ , ⟩`.** If the vectors have a single dimension it is simply
multiplication: `f_u × f_j`. With more dimensions, you multiply component by component
and sum. The property that matters: it is positive if the two numbers have the same
sign (same camp), negative if opposite. Example with `d=1`: a reviewer at `f_u = +2`, a
question at `f_j = +1.5` → contribution `+3`. That reviewer will give a high vote for
reasons of alignment, not quality.

---

## Regularization — and the asymmetric trick

**Problem.** With enough free parameters you can explain any data, including noise. It
is called *overfitting*: the model learns the instance, not the rule.

**Standard solution.** You add to the function-to-minimize a penalty proportional to
the size of the parameters:

```
L = error  +  λ · (sum of squared parameters)
```

The model now has to "pay" to use large numbers, so it uses them only if really
needed. Large `λ` = parameters squashed toward zero.

**The trick, which is the key to the whole system.** In our model there are two
families of parameters: the intercepts (`b`) and the factors (`f`). We set the penalty
on the intercepts much higher:

```
λ_b = 0.15        (intercepts: expensive)
λ_f = 0.03        (factors: cheap)
```

Practical meaning: for the model it is **expensive** to explain a high vote by saying
*"the item is objectively good"*, and **cheap** to explain it by saying *"that camp
likes the item"*. So the model always takes the ideological explanation first. What
ends up in the intercept `b_j` is only the consensus it **cannot** explain as
alignment.

That is why a high `b_j` means cross-cutting approval, and not "got a lot of votes".

---

## Bootstrap (statistical)

**Problem.** Does the result depend on a few lucky votes, or is it solid?

**Method.** You redo the estimate on random subsamples of the data, say ten times. If a
question's score stays high in all of them, it is robust. Taking the **minimum** of the
ten estimates means being deliberately pessimistic: an item passes only if it convinces
in every repetition.

# Part 2 — How the quality of a question is measured

## Psychometrics and the latent construct

**Latent construct**: the thing you want to measure but which is not directly
observable — competence, attitude, knowledge. You observe only the behaviors (the
answers) and work back from there.

**θ** (theta) is the standard symbol for the value of the construct in a person: their
competence. It is not a percentage, it is a scale centered on 0 with standard deviation
1 — so `θ = +1` means "one standard deviation above the mean".

---

## Item Response Theory (IRT)

**Problem with classical theory.** Saying "Marco scored 7 out of 10" is not
informative, because that 7 depends on how hard that particular test was. You cannot
compare people who took different tests, nor questions used on different populations.

**IRT solution.** You put people and questions on the *same* scale. Each question has a
**characteristic curve**: on the horizontal axis competence `θ`, on the vertical axis
the probability of answering correctly. The curve is S-shaped.

The three parameters are three ways that S can change:

**`b_j` — difficulty.** Where the S sits along the horizontal axis. The further right,
the harder the question. Precise definition: the level of `θ` at which you have a 50%
probability of answering correctly.

**`a_j` — discrimination.** How steep the S is. Steep = the question sharply separates
those who know from those who don't: below a certain competence almost everyone gets it
wrong, above it almost everyone gets it right. Flat = the probability of answering
correctly changes little with competence, so **the question is measuring nothing**. A
low `a` is the worst defect a question can have: it is not a wrong question, it is
noise.

**`c_j` — pseudo-guessing.** The height of the "floor" to the left of the curve. With
four options, someone who knows absolutely nothing still has about a 25% chance. If `c`
comes out much higher, something in the question hints at the answer.

---

## Point-biserial correlation (`r_pbis`)

A simple version of discrimination, computable without IRT: the correlation between
"answered *this* question correctly" (value 0 or 1) and "total score on the test".

If it comes out **negative** you have found a serious problem: those who do well on the
overall test get this question wrong. In practice this almost always means the answer
key is wrong, or the question is ambiguous and competent people see a complication that
is not there.

---

## Infit and Outfit MNSQ

They measure how far the observed answers deviate from those the model predicted. The
expected value is **1**.

- Above 1.3 → too much noise: the question measures something different from the rest of
  the test
- Below 0.7 → too predictable: the question is redundant, adds no information

*Outfit* is sensitive to isolated anomalous answers; *infit* weights more the answers
of people near the question's difficulty.

---

## DIF — Differential Item Functioning

**It is the political-neutrality mechanism of the whole system.** It is worth
understanding well.

**What it is NOT.** It is not "group A answers worse than group B". That can be a plain
difference in competence, and is not the question's fault.

**What it is.** **At equal competence `θ`**, two people from different groups have
different probabilities of answering correctly. If that happens, the question is picking
up group membership, not knowledge.

**Concrete example.** A question about the approval process of the budget law, phrased
with vocabulary that one political side uses daily and the other does not. Two people
who know the subject equally well: one grasps the question instantly, the other has to
decode it. The item has DIF, and no human review would necessarily have noticed.

**Two variants:**
- **Uniform DIF** — the advantage is constant at all competence levels (in the model it
  is the coefficient `β₂`)
- **Non-uniform DIF** — the advantage changes with competence (coefficient `β₃`)

**Interaction term.** `β₃(θ · f)` is the product of two variables inside a regression.
It says: "the effect of membership depends on the competence level". Without that term
you would implicitly assume the advantage is identical for someone who knows nothing and
for experts, which is often false.

---

## Mantel–Haenszel and ETS classification

**Mantel–Haenszel** is the standard statistical test for detecting uniform DIF. **ETS**
is the Educational Testing Service, the US body that produces the GRE and TOEFL; it
codified the A/B/C thresholds now used everywhere in professional testing.

`Δ_MH` is the effect size on a conventional scale called "delta". A `|Δ_MH| ≥ 1.5` is
class C, and in serious exams the item is simply discarded. It is not a threshold we
invented: it is the industry standard.

---

## Anchor items and iterative purification

**The problem is circular.** To establish whether an item is biased you need people's
competence `θ`. But you compute `θ` from the answers to items — including the biased
ones, which therefore pollute the very measure you are judging them with.

**Iterative solution:**

```
1. estimate θ using all items
2. identify the items with DIF
3. remove them
4. re-estimate θ using only the "clean" items  (= the anchor items)
5. re-test all items with the new θ
6. repeat until the set of discarded items stops changing
```

Without this step the neutrality test is unreliable in a subtle way, and it is a very
common mistake.

---

## Mixture IRT / latent classes

**Problem.** Classical DIF requires knowing which group each person belongs to. With
anonymous nodes you do not.

**Solution.** Instead of declaring the groups, you let the model discover that the
population splits into `G` subgroups that answer in systematically different ways. The
groups have no label — you do not know whether class 1 is "party X voters" — but **you
do not need to know**. To discard an item it is enough to establish that it behaves
differently on coherent subpopulations at equal competence.

`G` is chosen with information criteria like BIC, which balance how well the model
explains the data against how many parameters it uses to do so.

# Part 3 — Bayesian statistics

## Prior and posterior

- **Prior**: what you believe *before* seeing the data
- **Posterior**: what you believe *after* seeing them

The Bayesian approach consists of updating the former in light of the data to obtain the
latter. The practical advantage here: it naturally handles the "I have very little data
on this node" case.

## Beta distribution

The natural distribution for representing uncertainty about a proportion — a number
between 0 and 1. `Beta(α, β)` reads very intuitively: **"as if you had already observed
α successes and β failures"**.

`Beta(2, 3)` is therefore a weak prior that says "I believe the success rate is around
40%, but I am very uncertain and easily convinced by the data".

## Shrinkage

**Problem.** An author has 2 items accepted out of 2 → 100%. Another has 180 out of
200 → 90%. Who is better? Obviously the second, but the raw average says the opposite.

**Solution.** Shrinkage pulls estimates based on little data toward the overall mean.
With a `Beta(2,3)` prior:

```
author A:   (2 + 2)   / (2 + 3 + 2)   = 4/7     ≈ 57%
author B:   (2 + 180) / (2 + 3 + 200) = 182/205 ≈ 89%
```

Now the second wins, correctly. And the more data you accumulate, the less the prior
sways you: with 2000 items the prior is irrelevant. It is the same mechanism by which a
restaurant with a single 5-star review should not top the ranking.

## Hierarchical model

A two-tier model: each author has a latent ability `ψ_a`; each of their items has a
quality drawn from a distribution centered on `ψ_a`. It is needed because the items of
the same author are **not independent** — they share the style, habits, and defects of
whoever wrote them, and treating them as independent observations would inflate
confidence in the estimates.

# Part 4 — Incentives: how honesty is rewarded

## Proper scoring rule

**Problem.** You ask someone: "what probability do you give this event?". If you reward
them with the wrong formula, it pays them to lie — for instance to always declare
absolute certainty to look more competent.

**Definition.** A scoring rule is *proper* when the strategy that maximizes expected
gain is **declaring your true probability**. Honesty stops being a required virtue and
becomes the convenient choice. It is a mathematical result, not a wish.

**The two most used:**

```
Brier:  (p − outcome)²                            squared error, lower is better
Log:    ln(p) if it happens,  ln(1−p) if not      very harsh punishment for confident wrongness
```

With the log score, declaring 99% and being wrong costs enormously. It is why it is used
when overconfidence is the vice to discourage.

## Brier Skill Score

**Residual problem.** The raw Brier rewards predicting the obvious. If 90% of questions
get rejected, someone who always says "it will be rejected" gets an excellent score
without knowing anything.

**Solution.** You normalize against a reference — here, the crowd's average prediction:

```
BSS = 1 − (your error / baseline error)
```

- `BSS = 0` → you add nothing over following the majority
- `BSS > 0` → you are right **when the crowd is wrong**

This is the property we need: in a system that must resist majority capture, the correct
dissenter must be rewarded structurally, not out of the designer's goodwill. The concept
comes from meteorology, where forecasters have been evaluated methodically for decades.

## Peer prediction

**Problem.** Some dimensions never receive an objective verdict: formal clarity, source
quality, tone. How do you reward honesty when **no verifiable truth** exists to compare
against?

**Basic idea.** If you tell the truth and the others tell the truth, your judgments
correlate. If you lie, they correlate less. So you pay based on agreement with a
randomly chosen reviewer.

**Flaw of raw agreement.** It also incentivizes the lazy equilibrium "let's all always
say yes", which produces perfect agreement and zero information.

**Dasgupta–Ghosh solves it thus:**

```
score = agreement on an item you both judged
      − agreement on two items you judged separately
```

The second term estimates *baseline* agreement, the one you would get by chance or from
shared bias. Subtracting it leaves only informative agreement — the one arising from
having actually observed the same object. Technical result: telling the truth is the
highest-paying equilibrium among symmetric strategies.

## Bayesian Truth Serum (Prelec, 2004)

Each person declares **two** things: their own answer, and their prediction of how the
others will answer. The **surprisingly common** answer is rewarded — more frequent than
the group itself predicted.

**Why it works.** Someone who knows a true but little-known fact *also* knows it is
little-known, so in their prediction they underestimate how many others know it. If many
informed people do the same, that answer turns out more common than predicted. That
surprise is the signal.

It is the only known mechanism that surfaces an informed minority **without** having an
independent way of knowing it is right.

## Sublinear weight / quadratic funding

An idea originating from public-goods funding mechanisms (Buterin, Hitzig, Weyl). If a
group's weight grows as the **square root** of its size rather than linearly,
coordinating pays much less:

```
500 coordinated nodes  →  √500 ≈ 22 independent nodes
```

The practical difficulty is not the formula, it is **identifying who is coordinated** —
and that is done by looking at behavior correlation.

## Spectral clustering

A method for grouping elements from a similarity matrix — here: how similarly two
reviewers vote. It is called "spectral" because it operates on the eigenvectors of that
matrix. The practical result: it identifies groups of nodes behaving as a single block,
which is exactly what we need to apply the discount above.

# Part 5 — Protocol and cryptography

## Hash function

Turns data of any length into a fixed-length string, with two properties: you cannot go
back to the original, and you cannot find two different inputs with the same output. It
is the fingerprint of a piece of data.

## Commit-reveal

Two phases.

1. **Commit** — you publish the *hash* of your vote. You have committed and can no
   longer change it, but nobody knows what you voted.
2. **Reveal** — once the phase is closed you reveal the vote, and anyone verifies it
   matches the previously published hash.

It prevents copying others and adapting your judgment to what is emerging.

## Merkle tree and transparency log

**Merkle tree.** You organize the hashes into a tree: a single hash at the top (the
"root") summarizes millions of records. Changing a single record changes the root, and
changes it visibly.

**Transparency log.** An *append-only* register — you can only add, never modify or
delete — whose roots are periodically published and signed by independent witnesses.
Born for HTTPS certificates (Certificate Transparency), after some certificate
authorities issued fake certificates without anyone noticing for months.

**Why it is relevant here.** You get the property *"nobody can rewrite the past without
it being visible"* without needing a blockchain, at costs and latencies orders of
magnitude lower.

## Content-addressed storage

A file is identified by the hash of its own content rather than by a path. Consequence:
the address **guarantees** the content. Nobody can substitute the text of a question
while leaving the reference it is cited by unchanged.

## BFT — Byzantine Fault Tolerance

A family of consensus protocols that keep working correctly even if a minority of
participants (typically up to a third) do not merely fail, but **actively lie and
sabotage**. The name comes from Lamport's "Byzantine generals problem".

## Zero-knowledge proof

Proving you know or possess something **without revealing what**. Here: proving "I have
a valid credential attesting I am a unique person" without showing *which* credential,
and therefore without becoming identifiable.

## Blind signature

The issuer signs a message **without seeing its content**. So it can certify you but
cannot then recognize that message when it meets it in circulation. It is the historical
building block of anonymous electronic cash.

## BBS+ and anonymous credentials

A signature scheme that allows *selective disclosure*: you have a credential containing
many attributes and can prove only what is needed — "I am of age" — without exposing the
rest, and without two presentations of the same credential being linkable to each other.

## Nullifier

A value derived deterministically from your secret key and a "context":

```
nullifier = H(secret_key, context)
```

Three properties, all necessary:

1. **Always equal** for the same context → you cannot vote twice
2. **Not traceable to you** → nobody works back to the key
3. **Different for different contexts** → your author-ID and your evaluator-ID are not
   linkable to each other

The "context" is literally a string. In our design: `"author"` and `"evaluator"`. That
is how the two separate pseudonyms are obtained.

# Part 6 — Governance

## Stratified sortition

Random selection, but with the guarantee that each stratum is represented
proportionally. In our case the strata are not demographic — we do not know them — but
are the **positions on the latent axis `f_u`**.

An ancient practice (Athens drew lots for most offices) and today revived in
deliberative citizens' assemblies.

**Why we need it.** Whoever controls the parameters and the golden items effectively
controls the evaluators' score. If those people were *chosen* — even by the most
reputable nodes — meta-level capture would be trivial. Sortition removes the channel.

## Whitewashing and the Friedman–Resnick result

**Whitewashing**: abandoning an identity with a ruined reputation and starting over with
a new one.

**The result** (Friedman & Resnick, 2001, *The Social Cost of Cheap Pseudonyms*): if
pseudonyms are free, the mechanism is **forced** to treat all newcomers with suspicion.
That suspicion has a cost — honest people penalized on entry — and it is an
**unavoidable** cost, not an implementation defect fixable with a cleverer algorithm.

It is why the non-rotatability of the ID is a requirement and not a preference.

## Stylometry

Identifying the author of a text from statistical features of the writing: sentence
length, use of function words, punctuation, lexical preferences. It works surprisingly
well already with a few thousand words, and does not require the author to make
mistakes.

It is why questions must be automatically normalized before publication: 200 questions
written by the same person are more than a sufficient sample.

## Epistocracy

A system in which political weight depends on competence. Jason Brennan defends it in
*Against Democracy*; David Estlund criticizes it in *Democratic Authority* with the
demographic objection — even a perfectly neutral test weights participation on a
variable correlated with education, income, and native language.

It is not a technical concept but a political one, and it is worth keeping separate from
the rest: the infrastructure we are designing works identically in contexts where this
debate does not arise.
